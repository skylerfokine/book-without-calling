## SKYLER · Slide 1 · Title (20 s)

Hi, we're Book Without Calling, in the Care Delivery track.

Our one sentence: for uninsured adults in rural Appalachian Ohio counties like Athens and Meigs, who depend on a free mobile health van, we help them actually make their follow-up visits, by replacing phone tag with one-word texts, postcards and a local messenger. All of it works with no signal, no data plan, and no phone call.

## SKYLER · Slide 2 · The problem (45 s)

Across rural Ohio, about one in three households in our 32 Appalachian counties can't get even the FCC's minimum internet speed. And rural Ohio counties have 42 primary care providers per 100,000 people, against 75 statewide.

You can see it right here. In Athens County, 52 percent of homes can't get basic internet, and out in the rural parts it's 80 to 90 percent with no broadband at all. One county south, Meigs has 13.6 providers per 100,000, zero hospitals, and KFF calls it a health "dead zone."

So people rely on free mobile health vans. But when the doctor says "come back next month," booking still takes phone calls, from a clinic number, during work hours, to someone with no signal at home.

How many? In just these two counties, about 18,000 people live in poverty, in places where up to half of homes lack basic internet. And the same pattern runs across all 32 Appalachian counties.

So it has to work with no signal, no data, and no phone call.

## SKYLER · Slide 3 · Today's journey (35 s)

Meet Ray. He's a composite: 58, living outside Coolville in rural Athens County, uninsured, on blood pressure medication. He lives up a hollow with no reliable signal.

At the van, the doctor tells him to come back in a month. Then the clinic calls to book. Voicemail. Ray calls back from town on his lunch break. Phone tag.

Here's the breakdown: he has no signal at home, so he can't reply. And the day before, his truck won't start, and nobody at the clinic knows.

Result: Ray misses the visit and his meds lapse. His slot sits empty, and the nurse spent hours on the phone for nothing.

One thing we're still assuming: how many misses are people we never reached, versus people who couldn't get there. We'll come back to that.

## JAYANT · Slide 4 · Our solution (40 s)

Here's what changes. Ray books his follow-up before he leaves the van. Reminders come the way he picks: text, postcard, or an automated call that works on a landline.

He answers with one word like "1", a date like "can I come Jan 15th", or plain language like "truck broke down", and the system understands it. If he has no signal, the text just waits and sends when he gets to town. If he never replies, a paid local health messenger finds him at church or the Dollar General.

Day to day, one nurse runs one screen. And the AI never gives medical advice: anything medical or unclear goes to a human, and urgent words get "call 911" plus a red alert.

## JAYANT · Slide 5 · Data: use and storage (30 s)

Two questions we expect about data.

How much data does it use? For the patient, none: it's plain texts and calls. The van tablet saves offline and syncs on the Athens office Wi-Fi. Everything heavier, the AI, scheduling and the staff screen, runs on the clinic's office internet, never the patient's.

Where is patient information stored? In the clinic's own scheduling system, through a HIPAA-covered texting vendor: encrypted, staff login only. Athena stays the medical record; staff paste the daily list in once a day. Texts never carry health details, the tablet is cleared after syncing, and our reports are counts by ZIP code, never names.

Now let's show you.

## ABYGAIL · Slide 7 · Assumptions and risks (30 s)

Our riskiest assumption is that most missed follow-ups happen because nobody reached the person, not because they couldn't get there. The pilot tests that directly: every miss gets logged as "no reply" or "can't get there." If it's mostly transport, we shift to moving stops and partnering with transit, and the transport map we showed already collects that data.

The biggest risk to patients is the AI getting something wrong, or someone getting left out. So the AI only does scheduling, anything medical goes to a nurse, every automatic change sends a confirmation they can undo, and texts never contain health details. People with no phone still have postcards, automated calls, the messenger, and walk-ins.

## ABYGAIL · Slide 8 · Pilot and ask (40 s)

The smallest pilot that would prove this: three months on an Athens-based free mobile clinic route, at two rural Athens County stops, Coolville and Glouster. It's run by the van's own nurse or scheduler on one screen, plus one part-time health messenger.

We'd measure follow-up completion against the three months before, and keep going if no-shows drop by a third and booking calls drop by half. That's realistic: rural clinics adding text reminders alone have cut no-shows from 11 to 7 percent.

After the pilot, our team stays on: we set it up, train staff in an hour, and review the numbers with the clinic every week. Rural health partners carry it: the clinic owns the patient relationship, Ohio's Rural Health Transformation Program funds year one, and the messenger can certify as a community health worker for Medicaid billing.

To start, we'd love an introduction to a mobile clinic team in Athens County.

## ABYGAIL · Slide 9 · Success + close (15 s)

Success: after the pilot, follow-ups up and booking calls cut in half. After a year, every stop in Athens County and its neighbors. At scale, all 32 Appalachian counties, and a model for rural Ohio.

Thank you. We're happy to take questions.
