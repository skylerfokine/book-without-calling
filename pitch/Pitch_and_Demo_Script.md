## Slide 1 · Title (20 s)

Hi, we're Book Without Calling, in the Care Delivery track.

Our one sentence: for uninsured adults in rural Athens County, and the Appalachian counties around it, who depend on a free mobile health van, we help them actually make their follow-up visits, by replacing phone tag with one-word texts, postcards and a local messenger. All of it works with no signal, no data plan, and no phone call.

## Slide 2 · The problem (50 s)

We're here in Athens County, so let's start at home. Fifty-two percent of Athens County households can't get even the FCC's minimum internet speed. Out in the rural parts of the county, an OU and Buckeye Hills study found 80 to 90 percent of homes have no broadband at all. And about one in five people here live below the poverty line.

Go one county south, to Meigs, and it gets more extreme: 13.6 primary care providers per 100,000 people, against 75 statewide, zero hospitals, and KFF named it one of Ohio's health "dead zones." And this isn't just us: across Ohio's 32 Appalachian counties, about a third of households lack minimum-speed internet, and rural Ohio has barely half the primary care providers of the state overall.

So people rely on free mobile health vans. But when the doctor says "come back next month," booking still takes phone calls, from a clinic number, during work hours, to someone with no signal at home.

How many? In Athens County alone, about 13,600 people live in poverty and about 11,600 homes lack minimum-speed internet. Add Meigs and it's well over 15,000 people in two counties.

*[Read the quote you collected.]*

So our design has to work with no signal, no data, and no phone call.

## Slide 3 · Today's journey (40 s)

Meet Ray. He's a composite: 58, living near Coolville in rural Athens County, uninsured, on blood pressure medication. He lives up a hollow with no reliable signal.

At the van, the doctor tells him to come back in a month. Then the clinic calls to book. Voicemail. Ray calls back from town on his lunch break. Phone tag.

Here's the breakdown: he has no signal at home, so he can't reply. And the day before, his truck won't start, and nobody at the clinic knows.

Result: Ray misses the visit and his meds lapse. His slot sits empty, and the nurse spent hours on the phone for nothing.

One thing we're still assuming: how many misses are people we never reached, versus people who couldn't get there. We'll come back to that.

## Slide 4 · Our solution (45 s)

Here's what changes. Ray books his follow-up before he leaves the van. Reminders come the way he picks: text, postcard, or an automated call that works on a landline.

He answers with one word, like "1", "CHANGE", or plain language like "truck broke down", and the system understands it. If he has no signal, the text just waits and sends when he gets to town. If he never replies, a paid local health messenger finds him at church or the Dollar General.

Day to day, one nurse runs one screen. And the AI never gives medical advice: anything medical or unclear goes to a human, and urgent words get "call 911" plus a red alert.

Let us show you.

## Slide 6 · Assumptions and risks (35 s)

Our riskiest assumption is that most missed follow-ups happen because nobody reached the person, not because they couldn't get there. The pilot tests that directly: every miss gets logged as "no reply" or "can't get there." If it's mostly transport, we shift to moving stops and partnering with transit, and the transport map we showed already collects that data.

The biggest risk to patients is the AI getting something wrong, or someone getting left out. So the AI only does scheduling, anything medical goes to a nurse, every automatic change sends a confirmation they can undo, and texts never contain health details. People with no phone still have postcards, automated calls, the messenger, and walk-ins.

## Slide 7 · Pilot and ask (40 s)

The smallest pilot that would prove this: three months on an Athens-based free mobile clinic route, with one stop in rural Athens County and one in Pomeroy in Meigs County, our hardest case. If it works there, it works anywhere in the region. It's run by the van's own nurse or scheduler on one screen, plus one part-time health messenger. They need a texting number, postcards, and an hour of training.

We'd measure follow-up completion against the three months before. We keep going if no-shows drop by a third and booking calls drop by half. That's realistic: rural clinics adding text reminders alone have cut no-shows from 11 to 7 percent, and from 15 to 9.

After the pilot: Ohio's Rural Health Transformation Program has $202 million this year and over a billion over five years for exactly this. The messenger can certify as a community health worker for Medicaid billing.

Our ask: an introduction to a mobile clinic team serving Athens and Meigs counties, so we can get their baseline and start.

## Slide 8 · Success + close (25 s)

Success after the pilot: follow-ups up, booking calls cut in half, and patients telling us they didn't have to call anybody.

After a year: every van stop in Athens County, then Meigs, Vinton and our other neighbors, with two messengers per county and no-shows under 10 percent.

At scale: all 32 Appalachian Ohio counties, and a ready-made model for Ohio's Rural Health Transformation work statewide. Rural Ohioans can book care from any phone, or none.

Thank you. We're happy to take questions.
